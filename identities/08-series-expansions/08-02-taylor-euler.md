### 8.2 Local Taylor Series (Euler Numbers)

While the geometric series in §8.1 describe the function's behavior in the domains $x < c$ and $x > c$, they fail to converge at the exact symmetry point. To describe the function's local geometry precisely around its center, we can expand it as a Taylor series in the canonical logarithmic coordinate. This expansion is governed by the classical **Euler numbers**.

For $x$ in the vicinity of $c$, the function can be expressed as:

$$
\boxed{\; f(x;r,c) = \frac{1}{2x} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} \bigl(r\log(x/c)\bigr)^{2n} \;}
$$

where $E_{2n}$ are the Euler numbers ($1, -1, 5, -61, 1385, \dots$). This series converges strictly within the interval:

$$
\left|\log\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)\right| < \frac{\pi}{2r} \quad \implies \quad c e^{-\pi/(2r)} < x < c e^{\pi/(2r)}
$$

**Derivation:**

The derivation is a direct application of the Rosetta Stone identity, translating the known Taylor series of the hyperbolic secant function into our framework.

1.  **Invoke the Rosetta Stone**: 
From §1.3, we have the fundamental identity:
$$
2x f(x;r,c) = \operatorname{sech}\bigl(r\log(x/c)\bigr)
$$
Let $z = r\log(x/c)$, so that $2xf(x) = \operatorname{sech}(z)$.

2.  **Apply the Maclaurin Series for Secant**:
The classical Maclaurin series expansion for the hyperbolic secant function around $z=0$ is known to be generated by the even-indexed Euler numbers:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} z^{2n}
$$
*(Note: The odd Euler numbers are zero, which reflects the fact that $\operatorname{sech}(z)$ is an even function.)*

3.  **Substitute and Rearrange**:
Substitute $z = r\log(x/c)$ back into the series:
$$
2x f(x;r,c) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} \bigl(r\log(x/c)\bigr)^{2n}
$$
Dividing both sides by $2x$ immediately yields the boxed formula.

4.  **Determine the Radius of Convergence**:
The radius of convergence for a Taylor series is determined by the distance from the expansion point to the nearest singularity (pole) of the function in the complex plane.
*   The function $\operatorname{sech}(z) = 1/\cosh(z)$ has poles where $\cosh(z) = 0$.
*   The roots of $\cosh(z)$ in the complex plane occur at purely imaginary values: $z = \pm i\frac{\pi}{2}, \pm i\frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots$
*   The distance from the origin $z=0$ to the nearest poles is exactly $|z| = \pi/2$.
Therefore, the series converges strictly for $|z| < \pi/2$. Substituting our definition of $z$:
$$
\left|r\log\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)\right| < \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \implies \quad \left|\log\left(\frac{x}{c}\right)\right| < \frac{\pi}{2r}
$$

**Significance:**

This local series is profoundly interconnected with the rest of the framework. It explicitly shows why the curvature at the peak (§2.1) depends on $r^2$ (via the $n=1$ term, $E_2 = -1$). Furthermore, the sequence of Euler numbers here is exactly what gave rise to the closed forms for the absolute logarithmic moments derived via Fourier transform in §5.9, confirming a deep analytic consistency.
