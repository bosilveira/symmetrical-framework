# 27.1 Wick Rotation and Euler Numbers

The geometric transformation that exchanges the continuous, real spatial domain (Circle A) with the discrete, complex spectral domain (Circle B) is the Wick rotation, $z \mapsto iz$. Algebraically, this maps the Maclaurin series of the hyperbolic secant to the trigonometric secant, generating a strict alternating sign on the combinatorial coefficients.

$$
\boxed{\; \operatorname{sech}(iz) = \sec(z) \quad \iff \quad \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!}(iz)^{2n} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n E_{2n}}{(2n)!}z^{2n} \;}
$$

### Exposition
This identity is the Taylor-series manifestation of the geometric quarter-turn on the complex torus. It demonstrates that the Euler numbers $E_{2n}$, which govern the local curvature of the symmetrical function (§9.2), strictly alternate in sign when projected onto the orthogonal spectral axis. This alternating sign $(-1)^n$ is the exact geometric source of the orientation factors (Atiyah-Bott signs) assigned to the discrete poles of the Mellin transform.

### Derivation

The derivation relies on the analytic continuation of the generating functions for the secant families.

**Step 1: The Hyperbolic Secant Series**
The hyperbolic secant function is an even analytic function, generated around the origin by the sequence of Euler numbers $E_{2n}$:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(w) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} w^{2n}
$$

**Step 2: Apply the Wick Rotation**
Let the argument be a purely imaginary variable, $w = iz$. Substitute this into the generating series:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(iz) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} (iz)^{2n}
$$

**Step 3: Algebraic Expansion of the Imaginary Unit**
Evaluate the power of the imaginary unit $i = \sqrt{-1}$:
$$
(iz)^{2n} = i^{2n} z^{2n} = (i^2)^n z^{2n} = (-1)^n z^{2n}
$$
Substitute this back into the series:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(iz) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^n E_{2n}}{(2n)!} z^{2n}
$$

**Step 4: Identify the Trigonometric Dual**
By the standard relations between circular and hyperbolic functions, $\operatorname{sech}(iz) = \frac{1}{\cosh(iz)} = \frac{1}{\cos(z)} = \sec(z)$. Therefore, the series derived in Step 3 is exactly the Maclaurin series for the trigonometric secant function. The Wick rotation perfectly toggles the alternating sign pattern, distinguishing the geometric properties of Circle A from Circle B.

# 27.2 Euler Polynomials and the Critical Line Symmetry

The classical Euler polynomials, $E_n(x)$, arise naturally within the framework when evaluated at exponential arguments corresponding to the canonical multiplicative coordinate. Furthermore, the exact center of symmetry for these polynomials identifies the critical line $\mathrm{Re}(s)=1/2$.

$$
\boxed{\; \frac{2e^{e^u t}}{e^t+1} = \operatorname{sech}(t/2)e^{(e^u - 1/2)t} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty E_n(e^u)\frac{t^n}{n!} \;}
$$
At the symmetry axis $e^u = 1/2$:
$$
\boxed{\; E_n(1/2) = 2^{-n} E_n, \qquad E_{2k+1}(1/2) = 0 \;}
$$

### Exposition
This identity proves that the multiplicative coordinate $e^u$ is natively embedded in the generating structure of the Euler polynomials. Crucially, it demonstrates that $e^u = 1/2$ acts as an absolute center of symmetry for the framework. At this point, the exponential drift term $e^{(e^u - 1/2)t}$ evaluates to $e^0 = 1$, and all odd-order Euler polynomials strictly vanish. This real-space evaluation point exactly mirrors the analytic critical line $\mathrm{Re}(s) = 1/2$ of the Riemann Zeta function.

### Derivation

**Step 1: The Euler Polynomial Generating Function**
The classical generating function for the Euler polynomials $E_n(x)$ is defined as:
$$
\frac{2e^{xt}}{e^t+1} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty E_n(x) \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$

**Step 2: Factor the Left-Hand Side**
We manipulate the exponential denominator to extract the hyperbolic secant function. Multiply the numerator and denominator by $e^{-t/2}$:
$$
\frac{2e^{xt} e^{-t/2}}{(e^t+1)e^{-t/2}} = \frac{2e^{(x-1/2)t}}{e^{t/2} + e^{-t/2}}
$$
Using the definition $\operatorname{sech}(z) = \frac{2}{e^z+e^{-z}}$ with $z = t/2$:
$$
\frac{2e^{(x-1/2)t}}{e^{t/2} + e^{-t/2}} = \operatorname{sech}(t/2) e^{(x-1/2)t}
$$

**Step 3: Substitute the Multiplicative Coordinate**
Substitute the scale-invariant coordinate $x = e^u$ (the natural mapping from the additive space to the multiplicative half-line) into both the polynomial sum and the factored generating function:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(t/2) e^{(e^u - 1/2)t} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty E_n(e^u) \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$
This gives the first boxed identity.

**Step 4: Evaluate at the Symmetry Axis**
Set the evaluation point to $e^u = 1/2$. The exponential term becomes $e^{(1/2 - 1/2)t} = e^0 = 1$. The generating function collapses purely to the hyperbolic secant evaluated at a half-argument:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(t/2) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty E_n(1/2) \frac{t^n}{n!}
$$
However, we know the standard Maclaurin series for the secant from §27.1 is $\operatorname{sech}(w) = \sum \frac{E_{2n}}{(2n)!} w^{2n}$. Setting $w = t/2$:
$$
\operatorname{sech}(t/2) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{E_{2k}}{(2k)!} \left(\frac{t}{2}\right)^{2k} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{E_{2k}}{2^{2k}} \frac{t^{2k}}{(2k)!}
$$
Matching coefficients between the two series:
*   For even powers $n = 2k$: $E_{2k}(1/2) = \frac{E_{2k}}{2^{2k}}$.
*   For odd powers $n = 2k+1$: the classical series has coefficient $0$, thus $E_{2k+1}(1/2) = 0$.

# 27.3 The Bernoulli-Euler Pole Structure

The geometric distinction between the Bernoulli numbers (which encode the Riemann Zeta function) and the Euler numbers (which encode the Dirichlet Beta function) is dictated entirely by a strict factor of 2 in their complex pole spacing.

The generating functions and their poles are:
$$
\text{Bernoulli } B_{2n}: \quad \frac{t}{e^t-1} \quad \implies \quad \text{Poles at } t = 2\pi i k \quad (\text{Spacing } 2\pi i)
$$
$$
\text{Euler } E_{2n}: \quad \operatorname{sech}(t/2)e^{-t/2} \quad \implies \quad \text{Poles at } t = \pi i (2k+1) \quad (\text{Spacing } 2\pi i, \text{ shifted by } \pi i)
$$

### Exposition
This structural distinction reveals that the transition from the $\zeta(2n)$ universe (bosonic, all integers) to the $\beta(2n+1)$ universe (fermionic, odd integers) is purely a matter of evaluating the torus holonomy at a full period versus a half-period. The Bernoulli poles occur at integer multiples of $2\pi i$, while the Euler/sech poles occur strictly at half-integer multiples of $2\pi i$. This factor of 2 isolates the separate cohomological circles of the framework.

### Derivation

The derivation requires identifying the complex singularities of the respective generating functions.

**Step 1: The Bernoulli Generating Function**
The classical generating function for the Bernoulli numbers is $g_B(t) = \frac{t}{e^t-1}$.
Poles occur where the denominator is zero, but the numerator is non-zero.
$$
e^t - 1 = 0 \quad \implies \quad e^t = 1 \quad \implies \quad t = 2\pi i k \quad \text{for } k \in \mathbb{Z} \setminus \{0\}
$$
The distance between consecutive poles is $2\pi i k - 2\pi i (k-1) = 2\pi i$.

**Step 2: The Euler Generating Function**
From §27.2, the generating function for the Euler numbers is isolated in the hyperbolic secant term: $g_E(t) = \frac{2e^t}{e^{2t}+1} = \operatorname{sech}(t) $.
Wait, the standard generation is $\operatorname{sech}(t) = \sum E_{2k} t^{2k} / (2k)!$.
Poles occur where $\cosh(t) = 0$.
$$
\cosh(t) = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2} = 0 \quad \implies \quad e^t = -e^{-t} \quad \implies \quad e^{2t} = -1
$$
$$
2t = i\pi(2k+1) \quad \implies \quad t = i\pi \frac{2k+1}{2} = i\pi(k + 1/2) \quad \text{for } k \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
If we use the argument $t/2$ (as natively derived in §27.2 for $E_n(x)$), the poles are at $t/2 = i\pi(k + 1/2)$, meaning $t = i\pi(2k+1)$.
The distance between consecutive poles is $i\pi(2(k+1)+1) - i\pi(2k+1) = 2\pi i$.

**Step 3: Structural Comparison**
Both functions share the exact same asymptotic pole spacing of $2\pi i$. However, the grid is shifted.
*   Bernoulli poles form a lattice containing the origin: $\{ \dots, -2\pi i, \mathbf{0}, 2\pi i, 4\pi i, \dots \}$.
*   Euler poles form a shifted lattice strictly avoiding the origin: $\{ \dots, -i\pi, \mathbf{i\pi}, 3i\pi, \dots \}$.
This phase shift of $i\pi$ distinguishes the full-period integration contour (yielding $\zeta$) from the half-period integration contour (yielding $\beta$) in the Mellin domain.

### Derivation

The derivation combines the classical field-theoretic factorization of the Dedekind Zeta function with the Mellin convolution theorems developed in Section 13.

**Step 1: Classical Factorization of the Dedekind Zeta Function**
By the standard theory of algebraic number fields, the Dedekind Zeta function for an Abelian extension $K/\mathbb{Q}$ is the product of all Dirichlet L-functions associated with the characters of the Galois group of the extension.
For the Gaussian field $K = \mathbb{Q}(i)$, the extension is quadratic, and the relevant characters are the principal character $\chi_1$ (generating $\zeta(s)$) and the non-trivial character modulo 4, $\chi_4$ (generating $\beta(s)$).

**Step 2: The Master Framework Substitutions**
From §25.1 and §25.2, we established that any L-function is the ratio of an arithmetically smoothed Mellin transform to the base geometric transform:
$$
\zeta(s) = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\zeta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \beta(s) = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\beta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)}

where $F_\zeta(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1+n^2 x^2}$ and $F_\beta(x) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k}{1+(2k+1)^2 x^2}$.

**Step 3: Form the Algebraic Product**
Multiply the two framework representations together:
$$
\zeta(s)\beta(s) = \left( \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\zeta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)} \right) \left( \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\beta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)} \right) = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\zeta\}(s) \cdot \mathcal{M}\{F_\beta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) \cdot \mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)}

**Step 4: Apply the Mellin Convolution Theorem**

Applying this theorem independently to the numerator and the denominator yields:

\zeta(s)\beta(s) = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F_\zeta \star_M F_\beta\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f \star_M f\}(s)}
$$
This completes the derivation, proving that L-function multiplication in the spectral $s$-plane corresponds exactly to the physical, spatial multiplicative convolution of their arithmetic lattices.
