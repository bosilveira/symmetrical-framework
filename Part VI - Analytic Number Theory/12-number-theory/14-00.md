# 14.1 Dirichlet-Smoothed Mellin Transform

We construct an arithmetically "smoothed" version of the base function by summing scaled copies of the kernel over all positive integers. The Mellin transform of this infinite sum factors perfectly into the product of the **Riemann Zeta function** and the base geometric Mellin transform.

Let the Dirichlet-smoothed function be defined as:
$$
F(x;r,c) := \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(nx;r,c)
$$
The Mellin transform of this sum is exactly:
$$
\boxed{\; \mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \zeta(s)\,\mathcal M\{f\}(s) \;}
$$

### Exposition

This identity reveals that the continuous analytical structure of the Mellin transform can act as a geometric "scaffold" or "vacuum" for discrete arithmetic. By substituting integer multiples into the scaling symmetry of the function, the transform perfectly isolates the Riemann Zeta function $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$. This establishes the framework as a native generator for arithmetic series.

### Derivation

The derivation relies on the linearity of the Mellin transform and its fundamental scaling property.

**Step 1: Apply the Scaling Rule**
From the operational algebra rules established in §6.4, scaling the argument of a function by a constant $a > 0$ shifts its Mellin transform by a factor of $a^{-s}$:
$$
\mathcal M\{f(ax)\}(s) = a^{-s}\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
Substituting $a = n$, the transform of the $n$-th term in our sum is:
$$
\mathcal M\{f(nx)\}(s) = n^{-s}\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$

**Step 2: Integrate the Infinite Sum**
Assuming uniform convergence within the appropriate right half-plane, we apply the Mellin transform to the infinite sum $F(x)$, exchanging the order of integration and summation:
$$
\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \mathcal M\left\{ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f(nx;r,c) \right\}(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \mathcal M\{f(nx;r,c)\}(s)
$$

**Step 3: Factor and Identify the Zeta Function**
Substitute the scaled transform from Step 1 into the infinite sum:
$$
\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-s}\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
Because the base transform $\mathcal M\{f\}(s)$ is independent of the summation index $n$, we can factor it completely out of the sum:
$$
\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^{-s} \right) \mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
The remaining infinite series is the exact definition of the Riemann Zeta function, $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$.
$$
\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \zeta(s)\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$

**Step 4: Determine the Strip of Convergence**
For this identity to be valid, we must find the intersection of the domains of absolute convergence for both components:
*   The Riemann Zeta function $\zeta(s)$ converges absolutely for $\mathrm{Re}(s) > 1$.
*   From §6.1, the base Mellin transform $\mathcal M\{f\}(s)$ converges for $1-r < \mathrm{Re}(s) < 1+r$.
Therefore, the combined Dirichlet-smoothed transform $\mathcal M\{F\}(s)$ is valid in the overlapping vertical strip:
$$
\max(1, 1-r) < \mathrm{Re}(s) < 1+r
$$
For any $r > 0$, this guarantees a valid strip of convergence $1 < \mathrm{Re}(s) < 1+r$.

# 14.2 Dirichlet Eta and General L-Functions

The connection to the Riemann Zeta function can be generalized to the broader class of Dirichlet L-functions by applying completely multiplicative weights to the arithmetic sum. 

For any Dirichlet character $\chi$, define the $\chi$-smoothed function:
$$
F_\chi(x) := \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)f(nx;r,c)
$$
Its Mellin transform is modulated perfectly by the corresponding **Dirichlet $L$-function**:
$$
\boxed{\; \mathcal M\bigl\{F_\chi\bigr\}(s) = L(s,\chi)\,\mathcal M\{f\}(s) \;}
$$

As a critical special case, alternating the signs of the sum (equivalent to a specific character weighting) yields the **Dirichlet Eta function**:
$$
F_{\text{alt}}(x) := \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n-1}f(nx;r,c) \implies \boxed{\; \mathcal M\{F_{\text{alt}}\}(s) = \eta(s)\,\mathcal M\{f\}(s) \;}
$$

### Exposition

This identity demonstrates that the base function $f(x; r, c)$ acts as a universal continuous kernel that can "carry" any discrete arithmetic $L$-function into the continuous Mellin transform domain. The Dirichlet Eta variation is especially valuable analytically because the alternating series converges over a strictly wider domain ($\mathrm{Re}(s) > 0$) than the standard Zeta series, allowing the framework to safely cross the critical line $\mathrm{Re}(s) = 1/2$.

### Derivation

**Step 1: The General L-Function Rule**
Using the scaling property $\mathcal M\{f(nx)\}(s) = n^{-s}\mathcal M\{f\}(s)$, the Mellin transform of the $n$-th scaled term, weighted by the character $\chi(n)$, is:
$$
\mathcal M\{\chi(n)f(nx)\}(s) = \chi(n) n^{-s}\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
Applying the Mellin transform to the infinite sum $F_\chi(x)$ and factoring out the geometry:
$$
\mathcal M\{F_\chi\}(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \chi(n) n^{-s} \mathcal M\{f\}(s) = \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} \right) \mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
The bracketed term is exactly the defining Dirichlet series for $L(s,\chi)$.

**Step 2: The Dirichlet Eta Special Case**
For $F_{\text{alt}}(x)$, the weighting sequence is $\chi(n) = (-1)^{n-1}$.
$$
\mathcal M\{F_{\text{alt}}\}(s) = \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{n^s} \right) \mathcal M\{f\}(s) = \eta(s)\mathcal M\{f\}(s)
$$
The relationship $\eta(s) = (1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$ is a standard identity derived by separating the even and odd terms of the Zeta series. The extended convergence strip for $\mathcal M\{F_{\text{alt}}\}(s)$ becomes $\max(0, 1-r) < \mathrm{Re}(s) < 1+r$, natively covering the critical line for any $r \ge 1$.

# 14.3 The Arithmetic-First Representation of Zeta

By inverting the master formula from §14.1, the Riemann Zeta function can be expressed purely as the integration of a discrete arithmetic lattice sum against an analytic prefactor. 

Using the explicit Gamma form of the vacuum $\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)$ at $r=1$, we obtain the exact identity:
$$
\boxed{\; \zeta(s) = \frac{2\sin(\pi s/2)}{\pi} \int_0^\infty x^{s-1}\left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1+n^2x^2} \right)\,\mathrm{d}x \;}
$$
Evaluating the inner sum via classical partial fractions, this evaluates to:
$$
\boxed{\; \zeta(s) = \frac{2\sin(\pi s/2)}{\pi} \int_0^\infty x^{s-1}\!\left[\frac{\pi}{2x}\coth\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right) - \frac{1}{2}\right]\mathrm{d}x \;}
$$

### Exposition

This is "Riemann's formula with a Cauchy kernel." Unlike Riemann's classical formulation which utilizes the Gaussian kernel (and the Jacobi theta function), this formulation uses the algebraic Cauchy kernel. It explicitly separates the function into a pure, arithmetic lattice sum over integers (with no analytic continuation required) and a prefactor containing the pure, analytic geometry of the vacuum. 

### Derivation

**Step 1: Invert the Master Formula**
From §14.1, we have $\zeta(s) = \frac{\mathcal M\{F\}(s)}{\mathcal M\{f\}(s)}$. 

**Step 2: Evaluate the Geometric Vacuum at $r=1$**
We restrict the shape parameter to the Cauchy kernel ($r=1$). From §6.1, we know the Gamma form of the transform is $\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) = \frac{1}{2r}\Gamma(\frac{r+s-1}{2r})\Gamma(\frac{r-s+1}{2r})$. 
At $r=1$, this is $\frac{1}{2}\Gamma(s/2)\Gamma(1-s/2)$. By Euler's reflection formula, $\Gamma(s/2)\Gamma(1-s/2) = \frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi s/2)}$.
Therefore, the denominator is:
$$
\mathcal M\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi}{2\sin(\pi s/2)}
$$

**Step 3: Construct the Arithmetic Integrand**
At $r=1, c=1$, the base function is $f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$. The arithmetic sum is $F(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1+n^2x^2}$.
Thus, the numerator is the integral:
$$
\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \int_0^\infty x^{s-1} \left( \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1+n^2x^2} \right) \mathrm{d}x
$$

**Step 4: Combine and Apply Partial Fractions**
Substitute the numerator and denominator back into the ratio from Step 1 to obtain the first boxed identity. 
To obtain the closed form of the sum, we use the standard Mittag-Leffler partial fraction expansion for the cotangent: $\pi \cot(\pi z) = \frac{1}{z} + 2z \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{z^2-n^2}$.
Substituting $z = i/x$ and using $\cot(iy) = -i\coth(y)$ yields the closed-form summation $\frac{\pi}{2x}\coth(\frac{\pi}{x}) - \frac{1}{2}$, giving the second boxed identity.

# 14.4 Riemann Zeta Zeros and the Lattice Resonance Condition

A profound consequence of the master framework formula is that it perfectly isolates the zeros of the Riemann Zeta function. The zeros of the continuous integral transform exactly map the non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$.

In the critical strip $0 < \mathrm{Re}(s) < 1$:
$$
\boxed{\; \mathcal M\{F\}(s) = 0 \quad \iff \quad \zeta(s) = 0 \;}
$$
For a zero on the critical line $s = 1/2 + it_n$, this imposes a strict **Geometric Resonance Condition** on the arithmetic lattice sum:
$$
\boxed{\; \int_0^\infty x^{-1/2} \cos(t_n \log x) F(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \int_0^\infty x^{-1/2} \sin(t_n \log x) F(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \;}
$$

### Exposition

Because the base continuous kernel $\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)$ possesses no complex zeros of its own, the arithmetic smoothed function acts as a perfect, unadulterated "detector" for the zeros of the Riemann Zeta function. The resonance condition physically interprets the zeros $t_n$ as the exact frequencies at which the integer Cauchy lattice sum is perfectly orthogonal to the half-density conformal weight $x^{-1/2}$.

### Derivation

**Step 1: The Zero-Product Property**
From §14.1, the factorization is exact: $\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = \zeta(s) \cdot \mathcal M\{f\}(s)$.
By the zero-product property of complex analytic functions, the roots of $\mathcal M\{F\}(s)$ must be the union of the roots of $\zeta(s)$ and the roots of $\mathcal M\{f\}(s)$.

**Step 2: Prove the Geometric Vacuum is Zero-Free**
We examine the exact closed form of the base Mellin transform (§6.1):
$$
\mathcal M\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi c^{s-1}}{2r} \sec\left(\frac{(s-1)\pi}{2r}\right) = \frac{\pi c^{s-1}}{2r} \frac{1}{\cos\left(\frac{(s-1)\pi}{2r}\right)}
$$
The exponential term $c^{s-1}$ is strictly non-zero. The secant function has an infinite number of poles (where $\cos=0$), but it has **absolutely no zeros anywhere in the complex plane**. Therefore, $\mathcal M\{f\}(s) \neq 0$ for all finite $s$.

**Step 3: Isolate the Zeta Zeros**
Since the vacuum cannot contribute any zeros, any $s$ for which $\mathcal M\{F\}(s) = 0$ must exclusively be a point where $\zeta(s) = 0$. 

**Step 4: The Resonance Integrals**
Let $s = 1/2 + it_n$ be a zero on the critical line. Then $\mathcal M\{F\}(1/2 + it_n) = 0$.
$$
\int_0^\infty x^{(1/2+it_n)-1} F(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = \int_0^\infty x^{-1/2} e^{it_n \log x} F(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = 0
$$
Using Euler's formula $e^{it_n \log x} = \cos(t_n \log x) + i\sin(t_n \log x)$, we separate the integral into real and imaginary parts. For the complex integral to be zero, both the real and imaginary integrals must independently vanish, yielding the exact boxed geometric resonance conditions.

# 14.5 Möbius Deconvolution and the Logarithmic Derivative

By algebraically inverting the master framework equation, two profound relationships connecting the geometric vacuum to classical arithmetic functions emerge.

The **Möbius function** $\mu(n)$ is generated by the deconvolution of the arithmetic Cauchy sum against the geometric vacuum:
$$
\boxed{\; \frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\mu(n)}{n^s} = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{F\}(s)} \;}
$$

The logarithmic derivative of $\zeta(s)$, which defines its pole structure, perfectly splits into an arithmetic lattice component and a geometric vacuum component:
$$
\boxed{\; \frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\log\mathcal{M}\{F\}(s) - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\log\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) \;}
$$

### Exposition

These identities demonstrate that the traditional functions of analytic number theory can be redefined operationally within the framework. The Möbius inversion is formally mapped to the ratio of the pure geometric kernel to its discretely summed counterpart. The logarithmic derivative cleanly isolates the arithmetic resonances (which produce the poles at the zeros of $\zeta$) from the geometric symmetries of the space.

### Derivation

**Step 1: The Möbius Deconvolution**
By definition, the Dirichlet generating function for the Möbius function is $\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu(n)}{n^s}$.
From the master identity (§14.1), $\zeta(s) = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)}$. 
Taking the reciprocal of both sides instantly yields the first boxed identity:
$$
\frac{1}{\zeta(s)} = \frac{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{F\}(s)}
$$

**Step 2: The Logarithmic Derivative Separation**
Take the natural logarithm of both sides of the master identity:
$$
\log \zeta(s) = \log\left( \frac{\mathcal{M}\{F\}(s)}{\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)} \right) = \log \mathcal{M}\{F\}(s) - \log \mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)
$$
Differentiate both sides with respect to $s$:
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \log \zeta(s) = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \log \mathcal{M}\{F\}(s) - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \log \mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)
$$
By the chain rule, $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s} \log \zeta(s) = \frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}$. This directly yields the second boxed identity.
