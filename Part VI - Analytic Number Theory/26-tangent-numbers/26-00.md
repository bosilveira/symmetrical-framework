# 26.1 Log-Cumulants and Tangent Numbers

The cumulants of the logarithmic variable $U = \log(X/c)$, where $X$ follows the normalized symmetrical distribution $\rho(x;r,c)$, evaluate to exact rational multiples of the powers of $\pi$. The coefficients of these rational multiples are the classical **Tangent numbers**.

Since the distribution is symmetric, all odd-order log-cumulants ($\kappa_1, \kappa_3, \kappa_5, \dots$) are strictly zero. For any integer $n \ge 1$, the even-order log-cumulants are:

$$
\boxed{\; \kappa_{2n} = T_{n}\left(\frac{\pi}{2r}\right)^{2n} \;}
$$

where $T_n$ are the Tangent numbers (sequence A000182 in the OEIS): $T_1=1, T_2=2, T_3=16, T_4=272, \dots$

### Exposition
The cumulants of a distribution characterize its shape (variance, kurtosis, etc.) more fundamentally than raw moments. This identity demonstrates that the shape of the symmetrical function in logarithmic space is not merely smooth, but rigidly structured by the combinatorics of the Tangent numbers. Because the Tangent numbers arise from the Maclaurin series of $\log\sec(z)$, this identity is a direct statistical consequence of the geometric vacuum $\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s)$ taking the form of a secant function.

### Derivation

The derivation extracts the cumulants from the Cumulant Generating Function (CGF), which is defined as the natural logarithm of the Moment Generating Function (MGF).

**Step 1: Define the CGF**
Let $U = \log(X/c)$. From the moment formulas, the MGF of $U$ evaluated at a real parameter $t$ is:
$$
M_U(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[e^{tU}\right] = \sec\left(\frac{\pi t}{2r}\right)
$$
The Cumulant Generating Function, $K(t)$, is the natural logarithm of the MGF:
$$
K(t) = \log(M_U(t)) = \log\left(\sec\left(\frac{\pi t}{2r}\right)\right)
$$

**Step 2: Expand the Maclaurin Series**
By definition in probability theory, the $m$-th cumulant $\kappa_m$ is the coefficient of $t^m/m!$ in the Maclaurin series expansion of $K(t)$:
$$
K(t) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} \frac{\kappa_m}{m!} t^m
$$
The classical Maclaurin series for $\log\sec(z)$ is known to be generated precisely by the Tangent numbers $T_n$:
$$
\log\sec(z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{T_n}{(2n)!} z^{2n}
$$
*(Note: The absence of odd powers of $z$ mathematically proves that $\kappa_{2n-1} = 0$, confirming perfect symmetry).*

**Step 3: Substitute and Match Coefficients**
Substitute the argument $z = \frac{\pi t}{2r}$ into the classical series expansion:
$$
K(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{T_n}{(2n)!} \left(\frac{\pi t}{2r}\right)^{2n} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{(2n)!} \left[ T_n \left(\frac{\pi}{2r}\right)^{2n} \right] t^{2n}
$$

**Step 4: Extract the Cumulant Formula**
By matching the coefficients of this series term-by-term with the general definition of $K(t)$ from Step 2, we directly extract the even-order cumulants:
$$
\kappa_{2n} = T_n \left(\frac{\pi}{2r}\right)^{2n}
$$
This completes the derivation.

# 26.2 The Riemann Zeta Function from Log-Cumulants

By utilizing Euler's relations between combinatorial sequences and the Riemann Zeta function, the log-cumulants of the symmetrical distribution can be expressed directly in terms of $\zeta(2n)$. 

For any integer $n \ge 1$, the $(2n)$-th log-cumulant evaluates to:

$$
\boxed{\; \kappa_{2n}\!\bigl(\log(X/c)\bigr) = \frac{(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)!}{2^{2n-1}\,r^{2n}} \zeta(2n) \;}
$$

### Exposition
This identity is a profound bridge between probability theory and analytic number theory. It proves that the values of the Riemann Zeta function at positive even integers are not abstract analytic quantities; they are exact, rational normalizations of the physical shape (the cumulants) of the log-transformed distribution. The entire sequence of $\zeta(2n)$ is tangibly embedded in the tail decay of the symmetrical probability measure.

### Derivation

The derivation relies on substituting classical Eulerian identities for the Tangent numbers into the formula established in §26.1.

**Step 1: Relate Tangent Numbers to Bernoulli Numbers**
The Tangent numbers $T_n$ are classically linked to the Bernoulli numbers $B_{2n}$ via the relation:
$$
T_n = (-1)^{n-1} \frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)B_{2n}}{2n} = \frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)|B_{2n}|}{2n}
$$

**Step 2: Relate Bernoulli Numbers to the Zeta Function**
Euler's famous formula connects the even-indexed Bernoulli numbers directly to the Riemann Zeta function evaluated at even integers:
$$
\zeta(2n) = \frac{(-1)^{n+1}(2\pi)^{2n}B_{2n}}{2(2n)!} = \frac{(2\pi)^{2n}|B_{2n}|}{2(2n)!}
$$
We solve this for the absolute value of the Bernoulli number:
$$
|B_{2n}| = \frac{2(2n)! \zeta(2n)}{(2\pi)^{2n}} = \frac{2(2n)! \zeta(2n)}{2^{2n}\pi^{2n}}
$$

**Step 3: Express Tangent Numbers via Zeta**
Substitute the expression for $|B_{2n}|$ into the formula for $T_n$ from Step 1:
$$
T_n = \frac{2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)}{2n} \left[ \frac{2(2n)! \zeta(2n)}{2^{2n}\pi^{2n}} \right]
$$
The $2^{2n}$ terms cancel. The factorial terms simplify as $\frac{(2n)!}{2n} = (2n-1)!$. This yields:
$$
T_n = \frac{2(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)! \zeta(2n)}{\pi^{2n}}
$$

**Step 4: Substitute into the Cumulant Formula**
Recall the log-cumulant formula from §26.1:
$$
\kappa_{2n} = T_n \left(\frac{\pi}{2r}\right)^{2n} = T_n \frac{\pi^{2n}}{2^{2n} r^{2n}}
$$
Substitute the Zeta expression for $T_n$ from Step 3:
$$
\kappa_{2n} = \left[ \frac{2(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)! \zeta(2n)}{\pi^{2n}} \right] \left( \frac{\pi^{2n}}{2^{2n} r^{2n}} \right)
$$
The $\pi^{2n}$ terms cancel perfectly. We group the remaining terms:
$$
\kappa_{2n} = \frac{2(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)! \zeta(2n)}{2^{2n} r^{2n}}
$$
Simplifying the power of $2$ in the denominator ($2 / 2^{2n} = 1 / 2^{2n-1}$) yields the final boxed identity:
$$
\kappa_{2n} = \frac{(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)!}{2^{2n-1} r^{2n}} \zeta(2n)
$$
This completes the derivation.

# 26.3 Statistical Extraction of Zeta Values

The algebraic relationship between the log-cumulants and the Riemann Zeta function provides a direct operational method to extract the values of $\zeta(2n)$ simply by computing the standard statistical moments of the logarithmic distribution.

For $n=1$ (Variance) and $n=2$ (Kurtosis component), the explicit extractions are:
$$
\boxed{\; \zeta(2) = \frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{2r^2}{3} \operatorname{Var}(\log(X/c)) \;}
$$
$$
\boxed{\; \zeta(4) = \frac{\pi^4}{90} = \frac{4r^4}{45} \kappa_4(\log(X/c)) \;}
$$

### Exposition
This confirms that the transcendental values of the Riemann Zeta function are physically measurable properties of the distribution's shape. If one were to draw empirical samples from a system governed by this framework, the measurement of the variance and the fourth cumulant of the log-transformed data directly yield the classical values of $\zeta(2)$ (the solution to the Basel problem) and $\zeta(4)$.

### Derivation

The derivation involves inverting the master cumulant equation from §26.2 to solve for $\zeta(2n)$ and evaluating the low-order cases.

**Step 1: Invert the Master Equation**
From §26.2, we have:
$$
\zeta(2n) = \frac{2^{2n-1} r^{2n}}{(2^{2n}-1)(2n-1)!} \kappa_{2n}
$$

**Step 2: Evaluate $n=1$ (The Basel Problem)**
For $n=1$, the cumulant $\kappa_2$ is strictly the variance of the distribution.
Substitute $n=1$ into the inverted equation:
$$
\zeta(2) = \frac{2^{2(1)-1} r^{2(1)}}{(2^{2(1)}-1)(2(1)-1)!} \kappa_2 = \frac{2^1 r^2}{(4-1) \cdot 1!} \kappa_2 = \frac{2r^2}{3} \kappa_2
$$
Recall from our statistical derivations (§12.2) that $\kappa_2 = \frac{\pi^2}{4r^2}$. Substituting this back in proves consistency:
$$
\frac{2r^2}{3} \left( \frac{\pi^2}{4r^2} \right) = \frac{2\pi^2}{12} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}
$$

**Step 3: Evaluate $n=2$**
For $n=2$, the cumulant $\kappa_4$ dictates the excess kurtosis.
Substitute $n=2$ into the inverted equation:
$$
\zeta(4) = \frac{2^{2(2)-1} r^{2(2)}}{(2^{2(2)}-1)(2(2)-1)!} \kappa_4 = \frac{2^3 r^4}{(16-1) \cdot 3!} \kappa_4 = \frac{8 r^4}{15 \cdot 6} \kappa_4 = \frac{8 r^4}{90} \kappa_4 = \frac{4 r^4}{45} \kappa_4
$$
Recall from §12.2 that $\kappa_4 = 2\kappa_2^2 = 2\left(\frac{\pi^2}{4r^2}\right)^2 = 2\left(\frac{\pi^4}{16r^4}\right) = \frac{\pi^4}{8r^4}$.
Substituting this back in proves consistency:
$$
\frac{4r^4}{45} \left( \frac{\pi^4}{8r^4} \right) = \frac{4\pi^4}{360} = \frac{\pi^4}{90}
$$
The extraction is rigorous and exactly matches standard analytic values.
