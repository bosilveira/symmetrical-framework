# 4.6 Solid of Revolution Properties (The "Infinite Tulip")

When the graph of the function $y = f(x;r,c)$ is revolved around the $x$-axis, it generates a solid of revolution resembling an infinitely long trumpet or tulip. This geometric object possesses a unique set of properties, being infinitely long yet potentially having both a finite volume and a finite surface area, making it more "well-behaved" than classical examples like Torricelli's Trumpet.

**Theorem:** The solid of revolution generated by $y = f(x;r,c)$ for $x \in (0, \infty)$ has:
1.  **Infinite Length** for all $r>0$.
2.  **Finite Volume** for all $r > 1/2$.
3.  **Finite Surface Area** for all $r > 1$.

### Exposition
This combination of properties is highly non-intuitive. For $r > 1$, one could theoretically "fill" the object with a finite amount of liquid and "paint" its surface with a finite amount of paint, even though the object stretches to infinity. This contrasts with Torricelli's Trumpet (Gabriel's Horn), generated by $y=1/x$, which has a finite volume but an infinite surface area. The faster decay of the function $f(x)$ for $r>1$ ensures the convergence of both integrals.

### Derivations

#### 1. Infinite Length
As proven in §4.3, the total arc length integral $\int_0^\infty \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2} \mathrm{d}x$ diverges because the integrand approaches 1 as $x \to \infty$.

#### 2. Volume of Revolution

**Step 1:** State the formula for the volume of a solid of revolution:
$$
V = \int_{0}^{\infty} \pi [f(x;r,c)]^2 \mathrm{d}x
$$
**Step 2:** Recognize the integral. This integral is $\pi$ times the $L^2$ norm of the function, which was calculated exactly in §3.7.
$$
\int_{0}^{\infty} [f(x;r,c)]^2 \mathrm{d}x = \frac{\pi}{4r^2 c \sin(\pi / 2r)}
$$
**Step 3:** State the result and convergence condition.
$$
V = \pi \left( \frac{\pi}{4r^2 c \sin(\pi / 2r)} \right) = \frac{\pi^2}{4r^2 c \sin(\pi / 2r)}
$$
The derivation of the $L^2$ norm integral requires the arguments of its underlying Beta function to be positive, which imposes the strict condition $r > 1/2$. For $r \le 1/2$, the volume is infinite.

#### 3. Surface Area of Revolution

**Step 1:** State the formula for the surface area of a solid of revolution:
$$
A = \int_{0}^{\infty} 2\pi f(x;r,c) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \mathrm{d}x
$$
**Step 2:** Analyze the convergence of the integral by examining the asymptotic behavior of the integrand at both boundaries ($x \to 0^+$ and $x \to \infty$).

*   **Behavior as $x \to \infty$**:
    From the asymptotic tails (§1.9), we know $f(x) \sim c^r x^{-r-1}$ and $f'(x) \to 0$. Therefore, the integrand behaves as:
    $$
    \text{Integrand} \sim 2\pi (c^r x^{-r-1}) \sqrt{1+0} \propto x^{-r-1}
    $$
    The integral $\int^\infty x^{-r-1} \mathrm{d}x$ converges if and only if $-r-1 < -1$, which implies $r>0$.

*   **Behavior as $x \to 0^+$**:
    From the asymptotic tails (§1.9), $f(x) \sim c^{-r} x^{r-1}$ and $f'(x) \sim (r-1)c^{-r}x^{r-2}$.
    If $r < 2$, $f'(x)$ diverges as $x \to 0^+$, so the term $\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2} \sim |f'(x)| \propto x^{r-2}$.
    The integrand near the origin behaves as:
    $$
    \text{Integrand} \sim 2\pi f(x) |f'(x)| \propto x^{r-1} \cdot x^{r-2} = x^{2r-3}
    $$
    The integral $\int_0 x^{2r-3} \mathrm{d}x$ converges if and only if the exponent $2r-3 > -1$, which implies $2r > 2$, or $r > 1$. (If $r \ge 2$, $f'(x)$ does not diverge at the origin, and the convergence is even stronger).

**Step 3:** Combine convergence conditions.
For the total surface area to be finite, the integral must converge at both ends. The condition from the origin ($r>1$) is stricter than the condition from infinity ($r>0$). Therefore, the surface area is finite if and only if $r>1$.

# 4.7 Arc Length at r=2 (Lemniscate Connection)

While the general arc-length integral is a hyperelliptic function, for the specific case $r=2$, the integral reduces to a classical elliptic integral that can be parametrized by the **lemniscate functions**. This provides a direct geometric link between the shape of the function at $r=2$ and the geometry of Bernoulli's lemniscate.

**Theorem:** The arc-length integral for $f(x;2,c)$ on the interval $[0,c]$ can be expressed as an elliptic integral in lemniscate coordinates.

### Exposition

The case $r=2$ is special throughout the framework, often corresponding to the Moonshine locus or critical quantum thresholds. Its unique geometric status is reflected here, as its arc length connects to the lemniscate constant $\varpi = \int_0^1 (1-t^4)^{-1/2} \mathrm{d}t$ and its associated functions, $\mathrm{sl}(u)$ and $\mathrm{cl}(u)$.

### Derivation

**Step 1:** State the function and its derivative for $r=2$.
$$
f(x;2,c) = \frac{c^2 x}{c^4+x^4}, \qquad f'(x;2,c) = \frac{c^2(c^4 - 3x^4)}{(c^4+x^4)^2}
$$

**Step 2:** Form the arc-length integral for the segment $[0,c]$.
$$
L_2(0,c) = \int_0^c \sqrt{1 + \left( \frac{c^2(c^4 - 3x^4)}{(c^4+x^4)^2} \right)^2} \mathrm{d}x
$$

**Step 3:** Apply the Lemniscate Substitution.
The structure of the function with $x^4$ suggests the lemniscate substitution $x = c \cdot \mathrm{sl}(u)$, where $\mathrm{sl}(u)$ is the lemniscate sine. As $x$ ranges from $0$ to $c$, the variable $u$ ranges from $0$ to $\varpi$ (the half-period of the lemniscate sine). The differential is $\mathrm{d}x = c \cdot \mathrm{sl}'(u)\mathrm{d}u = c \sqrt{1-\mathrm{sl}^4(u)}\mathrm{d}u$.

**Step 4:** Transform the integrand.
The term $f(x;2,c)$ itself has an elegant form under this substitution (as shown in your notes):
$$
f(c \cdot \mathrm{sl}(u); 2, c) = \frac{\mathrm{sl}(u)}{c(1+\mathrm{sl}^4(u))}
$$
The derivative $f'(x)$ becomes a complex function of $\mathrm{sl}(u)$ and $\mathrm{cl}(u)$. The full integrand transforms into the form:
$$
\text{Integrand} = R(\mathrm{sl}(u), \mathrm{cl}(u)) \sqrt{1-\mathrm{sl}^4(u)}
$$
where $R$ is a rational function.

**Step 5:** State the final integral form.
Let $s = \mathrm{sl}(u)$. The integral for the arc length becomes:
$$
L_2(0,c) = c \int_0^{\varpi} \frac{\sqrt{(1-s^4)\left[ (1+s^4)^4 + (1-3s^4)^2(1-s^4) \right]}}{(1+s^4)^2} \mathrm{d}u
$$
This is a genuine elliptic integral in the lemniscate parametrization. While it does not have a simple closed form, its representation confirms the deep geometric connection between the $r=2$ case and the classical theory of elliptic functions associated with the lemniscate.
