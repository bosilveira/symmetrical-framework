### 4.4 Euler-Operator Recurrence Relation

The differential structure of the function is particularly elegant when analyzed with the **Euler operator**, $D := x \, d/dx$. This operator is the natural derivative for systems with scaling symmetry, as it measures the change of a function with respect to a multiplicative (logarithmic) change in its argument.

Applying the Euler operator repeatedly to $f(x)$ does not create new transcendental functions; instead, it generates a sequence of polynomials in the companion function $W(x) = \tanh(r\log(x/c))$.

**Theorem:** For any integer $n \ge 0$,

$$
D^n f = f \cdot P_n(W)
$$

where the polynomials $P_n(W)$ are generated by the recurrence relation:

$$
\boxed{ P_{n+1}(W) = r(1-W^2)P_n'(W) - (1+rW)P_n(W) }
$$

with the initial condition $P_0(W) = 1$.

**Explicit Low-Order Polynomials:**

*   $P_0(W) = 1$
*   $P_1(W) = -1 - rW$
*   $P_2(W) = 1 - r^2 + 2rW + 2r^2W^2$
*   $P_3(W) = -1 + 3r^2 - 3rW - 6r^2W^2 + 5r^3W - 6r^3W^3$

**Derivation (Proof by Induction):**

We prove the theorem by mathematical induction on `n`.

*   **Base Case (n=0):** The statement is $D^0 f = f$, which by definition means $f = f \cdot P_0(W)$. This holds true for $P_0(W) = 1$.

*   **Base Case (n=1):** We apply the operator once: $D f = x f'$. From the derivation of the Master ODE (§4.1), we established the key identity $x f'/f = -1 - rW$. Therefore:

$$
Df = f \cdot (-1 - rW)
$$

This matches the form $f \cdot P_1(W)$ with $P_1(W) = -1-rW$. The theorem holds for n=1.

*   **Inductive Step:** Assume the theorem holds for some integer $k \ge 1$, i.e., $D^k f = f \cdot P_k(W)$. We must show that it holds for $k+1$. We apply the operator $D$ to the assumed form:

$$
D^{k+1}f = D(D^k f) = D(f \cdot P_k(W))
$$

Using the product rule for the operator $D$ ($D(ab) = (Da)b + a(Db)$), we get:

$$
D^{k+1}f = (Df)P_k(W) + f(D P_k(W))
$$

We analyze the two terms:
1.  $(Df)P_k(W) = f \cdot P_1(W) \cdot P_k(W) = f(-1-rW)P_k(W)$.
2.  For the second term, we use the chain rule: $D P_k(W) = x \frac{d}{dx}P_k(W(x)) = x P_k'(W) \frac{dW}{dx} = P_k'(W) \cdot (x \frac{dW}{dx})$. Let's call the last part $DW$. We compute $DW$ directly from the definition $W = \tanh(r\log(x/c))$:

$$
\frac{dW}{dx} = \mathrm{sech}^2(r\log(x/c)) \cdot \frac{r}{x} = (1 - W^2) \frac{r}{x}
$$

Therefore, $DW = x \frac{dW}{dx} = r(1-W^2)$. So, the second term is $f \cdot P_k'(W) \cdot r(1-W^2)$.

Combining these two parts:

$$
D^{k+1}f = f(-1-rW)P_k(W) + f \cdot r(1-W^2)P_k'(W)
$$

Factoring out $f$, we see that this is of the form $f \cdot P_{k+1}(W)$, where:

$$
P_{k+1}(W) = r(1-W^2)P_k'(W) - (1+rW)P_k(W)
$$

This is precisely the recurrence relation stated in the theorem. Thus, by the principle of mathematical induction, the theorem holds for all $n \ge 0$.
