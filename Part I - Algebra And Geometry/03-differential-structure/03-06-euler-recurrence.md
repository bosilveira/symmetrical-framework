# 3.6 Euler Operator Recurrence

The differential structure of the function is particularly elegant when analyzed with the **Euler operator**, $D_x := x \, \mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$. This operator is the natural derivative for systems with scaling symmetry. Applying the Euler operator repeatedly to $f(x)$ generates a sequence of polynomials in the conformal phase function $W(x)$.

**Theorem:** For any integer $n \ge 0$, the $n$-th application of the Euler operator to $f(x)$ has the form:

$$
\boxed{ D_x^n f = f \cdot P_n(W) }
$$

where the polynomials $P_n(W)$ are generated by the recurrence relation:

$$
\boxed{ P_{n+1}(W) = r(1-W^2)P_n'(W) - (1+rW)P_n(W) }
$$

with the initial condition $P_0(W) = 1$.

### Exposition

This theorem reveals a deep recursive structure within the differential properties of the function. It shows that the action of the scaling derivative operator, $D_x$, is algebraically closed on the function, always returning the function itself multiplied by a predictable polynomial in the phase variable $W$. This structure is characteristic of integrable systems and has connections to the theory of ladder operators in quantum mechanics.

### Derivation (Proof by Induction)

We prove the theorem by mathematical induction on `n`.

**Base Case (n=0):**
The statement is $D_x^0 f = f$, which by definition means $f = f \cdot P_0(W)$. This holds true for the initial condition $P_0(W) = 1$.

**Base Case (n=1):**
We apply the operator once: $D_x f = x f'$. The Euler-Cauchy identity from §3.2 can be rearranged to give an explicit expression for $xf'$:

$$
xf' = -(1+rW)f
$$

This matches the form $f \cdot P_1(W)$ with $P_1(W) = -1-rW$. The theorem holds for $n=1$. This also verifies the first step of the recurrence:

$$
P_1(W) = r(1-W^2)P_0'(W) - (1+rW)P_0(W) = r(1-W^2)(0) - (1+rW)(1) = -1-rW
$$

**Inductive Step:**
Assume the theorem holds for some integer $k \ge 0$, i.e., $D_x^k f = f \cdot P_k(W)$. We must show that it holds for $k+1$. We apply the operator $D_x$ to the assumed form:

$$
D_x^{k+1}f = D_x(D_x^k f) = D_x(f \cdot P_k(W))
$$

Using the product rule for the operator $D_x$ ($D_x(ab) = (D_x a)b + a(D_x b)$), we get:

$$
D_x^{k+1}f = (D_x f)P_k(W) + f(D_x P_k(W))
$$

We analyze the two terms separately:
1.  **First Term:** From the base case, we know $D_x f = f \cdot P_1(W) = -f(1+rW)$. So the first term is:

$$
(D_x f)P_k(W) = -f(1+rW)P_k(W)
$$

2.  **Second Term:** For the second term, we use the chain rule. $D_x P_k(W) = x \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}P_k(W(x)) = x P_k'(W) \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}x} = P_k'(W) \cdot (x \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}x})$.
From the Riccati equation in §3.4, we have $x \frac{\mathrm{d}W}{\mathrm{d}x} = r(1-W^2)$. So the second term is:

$$
f(D_x P_k(W)) = f \cdot P_k'(W) \cdot r(1-W^2)
$$

Combine these two results:

$$
D_x^{k+1}f = f \cdot r(1-W^2)P_k'(W) - f(1+rW)P_k(W)
$$

Factoring out $f$, we see that this is of the form $f \cdot P_{k+1}(W)$, where:

$$
P_{k+1}(W) = r(1-W^2)P_k'(W) - (1+rW)P_k(W)
$$

This is precisely the recurrence relation stated in the theorem. Thus, by the principle of mathematical induction, the theorem holds for all $n \ge 0$.
