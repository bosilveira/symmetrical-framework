# 33.1 The Euler-Secant Identity and Mittag-Leffler Expansion

The Mellin transform of the symmetrical function can be explicitly expanded into a sum of simple rational poles using the Mittag-Leffler theorem. This classical partial fraction expansion reveals that the discrete spectral data (living on Circle B) is strictly characterized by residues with an alternating sign sequence, $(-1)^n$.

The Mellin transform expands as:
$$
\boxed{\; \mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi}{2r}\sec\left(\frac{(s-1)\pi}{2r}\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \left[ \frac{(-1)^n}{s - s_n^+} + \frac{(-1)^n}{s - s_n^-} \right] \;}
$$
where the poles are located at the levels $s_n^\pm = 1 \pm (2n+1)r$.

### Exposition
This identity is the exact meromorphic structure of the geometric vacuum. The alternating sign $(-1)^n$ is not an ad hoc choice; it is mathematically forced by the half-period antisymmetry of the secant function ($\sec(z+\pi) = -\sec(z)$). In the context of equivariant geometry and Moonshine, these exact poles correspond to the mass levels of the theory, and the alternating residues provide the unyielding geometric orientation factors ($\pm 1$) for the Atiyah-Bott localization.

### Derivation

The derivation utilizes Euler's classical partial fraction expansion for the secant function.

**Step 1: Euler's Secant Expansion**
The classical Mittag-Leffler expansion for the secant function expresses it as an infinite sum over its simple poles:
$$
\sec(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{(2n+1)\pi}{(2n+1)^2 \frac{\pi^2}{4} - z^2}
$$

**Step 2: Substitute the Mellin Argument**
From §6.1, the Mellin transform is $\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi}{2r}\sec(z)$, with the argument $z = \frac{(s-1)\pi}{2r}$. 
Substitute this argument into Euler's expansion:
$$
\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi}{2r} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{(2n+1)\pi}{(2n+1)^2 \frac{\pi^2}{4} - \left(\frac{(s-1)\pi}{2r}\right)^2}
$$

**Step 3: Simplify the Denominator**
Factor out $\frac{\pi^2}{4r^2}$ from the denominator inside the sum:
$$
(2n+1)^2 \frac{\pi^2}{4} - \frac{(s-1)^2 \pi^2}{4r^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{4r^2} \left[ (2n+1)^2 r^2 - (s-1)^2 \right]
$$

**Step 4: Cancel Constants and Apply Difference of Squares**
Substitute the factored denominator back into the expression:
$$
\mathcal{M}\{f\}(s) = \frac{\pi}{2r} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{(2n+1)\pi}{\frac{\pi^2}{4r^2} \left[ (2n+1)^2 r^2 - (s-1)^2 \right]}
$$
$$
= \frac{\pi}{2r} \frac{4r^2}{\pi^2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{(2n+1)\pi}{ (2n+1)^2 r^2 - (s-1)^2 } = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} (-1)^n \frac{2r(2n+1)}{ (2n+1)^2 r^2 - (s-1)^2 }
$$
Apply the difference of squares factorization to the denominator: $A^2 - B^2 = (A-B)(A+B)$, where $A = (2n+1)r$ and $B = s-1$.
$$
(2n+1)^2 r^2 - (s-1)^2 = \bigl((2n+1)r - (s-1)\bigr)\bigl((2n+1)r + (s-1)\bigr)
$$
$$
= \bigl(1 + (2n+1)r - s\bigr)\bigl(s - 1 + (2n+1)r\bigr)
$$

**Step 5: Separate into Partial Fractions**
Using the identity $\frac{2A}{(A-B)(A+B)} = \frac{1}{A-B} + \frac{1}{A+B}$, with $A = (2n+1)r$ and $B=s-1$:
$$
\frac{2r(2n+1)}{ \bigl((2n+1)r - (s-1)\bigr)\bigl((2n+1)r + (s-1)\bigr) } = \frac{1}{(2n+1)r - (s-1)} + \frac{1}{(2n+1)r + (s-1)}
$$
$$
= \frac{1}{1 + (2n+1)r - s} + \frac{1}{s - (1 - (2n+1)r)}
$$
$$
= -\frac{1}{s - (1 + (2n+1)r)} + \frac{1}{s - (1 - (2n+1)r)}
$$
Let $s_n^+ = 1 + (2n+1)r$ and $s_n^- = 1 - (2n+1)r$. This yields the final partial fraction expansion. The alternating signs are structurally locked into the sum.

# 33.2 Orthogonality and the Residue Theorem

$$

### Exposition
This identity translates the geometric orthogonality of the torus into complex analysis. Circle A is the real axis ($u \in \mathbb{R}$), where the function $\mathrm{sech}(ru)$ is smooth and continuous. Circle B corresponds to the purely imaginary axis ($u \in i\mathbb{R}$), where the function $\mathrm{sech}(r(i\varphi)) = \sec(r\varphi)$ exhibits discrete poles. The residue theorem acts as the bridge proving that integrating along the continuous spectrum yields the exact same total weight as summing the discrete, alternating pole residues on the orthogonal axis.

### Derivation

**Step 1: Evaluate the Continuous Integral (Circle A)**
From the fundamental definitions (§5.3 and §1.10), the integral over the real line (Circle A) yields the total mass:
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(u; r) \,\mathrm{d}u = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{r}{\pi}\mathrm{sech}(ru) \,\mathrm{d}u = 1
$$
Without the probability normalization, the raw integral is:
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm{sech}(ru) \,\mathrm{d}u = \frac{\pi}{r}
$$

**Step 2: Identify the Poles (Circle B)**
We analytically continue the function to the complex plane. The poles of $\mathrm{sech}(rz)$ occur where $\cosh(rz) = 0$. These are purely imaginary points:
$$
z_n = i\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{2r} \quad \text{for } n \in \mathbb{Z}
$$
These points lie entirely on the imaginary axis (Circle B).

**Step 3: Evaluate the Discrete Sum of Residues**
From §10.1 (the contour derivation of Sech Self-Duality), the residue of $\mathrm{sech}(rz)$ at a pole $z_n$ is:
$$
\mathrm{Res}_{z=z_n} \mathrm{sech}(rz) = \frac{i}{r}(-1)^n

If we apply the Cauchy Residue theorem to a contour enclosing the upper half-plane poles ($n \ge 0$), we sum the residues. Ignoring the imaginary unit generated by the contour winding, the absolute sum of the residues forms the alternating series:
$$
\frac{2}{r} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}
$$

**Step 4: Invoke the Leibniz Series**
The infinite sum evaluates to the classical Leibniz series for $\pi/4$:
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}
$$
Substituting this back into the scaled sum yields:
$$
\frac{2}{r} \left( \frac{\pi}{4} \right) = \frac{\pi}{2r}
$$
Accounting for both the upper and lower half-plane poles recovers the full value $\pi/r$, exactly matching the continuous integral evaluated in Step 1. The discrete Circle B strictly governs the continuous Circle A.
